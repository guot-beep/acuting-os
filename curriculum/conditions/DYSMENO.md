Dysmenorrhea

Dysmenorrhea refers to severe, frequent cramping in the lower abdomen and lower back either before, during or after menstrual cycle.

Etiology & Pathogenesis

Cramps occur from contractions in the uterus, which are part of the menstrual process.

About half of menstruating women experience primary dysmenorrhea.  The causes of primary dysmenorrhea are related to:  1.  contraction-causing chemicals such as prostaglandins and arachidonic acid, which induce myometrium (uterine muscle) contractions.  2.  abnormal nervous system response - some women may have autonomic nervous systems that are overly sensitive to menstrual cycle changes (the autonomic nervous system regulates the heart rate, blood pressure, and it contains the pain receptors in nerve fibers in the uterus and pelvic area.).  3.  abnormalities in the arteries in the uterus - studies using a imaging technique called Doppler ultrasound report impaired blood flow through the arteries in the uterus in women with severe dysmenorrhea.  4.  genetic factors may play a critical role in over half of primary dysmenorrhea cases.  Onset usually occurs two to three years after the periods have started.  The pain typically develops when the bleeding starts and continues for 32 to 48 hours.
Secondary dysmenorrhea is menstrually related pain that accompanies with another medical or physical condition, usually endometriosis or the use of an intrauterine device (IUD), the presence of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), uterine fibroids, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, uterine polyps, or cancer.

The Risk Factors For Dysmenorrhea

1.  Adolescence and Early Puberty
    Those who start menstruating at age 11 or younger are at higher risk for severe pain, longer periods, and longer menstrual cycles.  In any case, between 20% and 90% of teenage girls report menstrual pain and about 15% report that it is severe.
2.  Being Overweight or Underweight
    Studies suggest that being underweight posed a higher risk for frequent menstrual pain than being overweight.  An earlier study reported, however, that women who are overweight have twice the risk for having severe and prolonged cramping as women who are not overweight.
3.  Smoking and Alcohol Use
    Smokers have a 50% higher risk than nonsmokers for menstrual pain.  In fact, studies have also reported a higher incidence of dysmenorrhea among women exposed to passive smoking.
    Alcohol does not cause menstrual pain, but in women with existing dysmenorrhea, alcohol consumption may prolong the cramps.
4.  Stress
5.  Chronic Pelvic Pain
    Many women experience chronic pain in the pelvic area, and in one study about 81% of these women also experience dysmenorrhea.  In this study, stress and irritable bowel syndrome (which is related to stress) were the most common causes of chronic pelvic pain.
