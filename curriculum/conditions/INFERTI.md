Western Prospective

Infertility refers to inability of a couple to conceive after one year of unprotected intercourse.  It affects about 1 of 5 couples in the USA.  Etiologic factors are sperm disorders (35% of couples), ovulatory dysfunction ( 20%), tubal dysfunction (30%), abnormal cervical mucus (5%), and unidentified factors (10%).  Diagnosis and treatment require thorough assessment of both partners.  The financial burden and time commitment for diagnostic and therapeutic infertility procedures can cause marital strife.  Failure to conceive often generates frustration, emotional stress, feelings of inadequacy, anger, guilt, and resentment.  Consequently, counseling and psychologic support are important adjuncts to treatment.  Despite all efforts, some couples do not achieve pregnancy.  They should be counseled about when to stop treatments and when to consider adoption.

Related Tests & Procedures

Semen Analysis:  A major test for evaluating male infertility, which should be performed after 2 to 3 days of sexual abstinence.  At least 2 to 3 ejaculates obtained at intervals of no less than a week should be examined because sperm count varies.  Normal sperm count >20 million/ml, sperm motility at 1 and 3 hours >50%, sperm morphology normal >60%.

Testicular Biopsy:  A procedure to assess function of the seminiferous tubules.

Ovulation Monitoring:  Daily measurements of basal body temperature have been used to monitor ovulation.  A nadir in temperature suggests impending ovulation; an increase of >0.5 C ( 0.9  F) characterizes the postovulatory period.  However, this method is not reliable or accurate; at best, it can predict ovulation only within 2 days.   Much more reliable are pelvic ultrasound monitoring of ovarian follicle diameter and ovulation predictor kits that detect an increase in urinary luteinizing hormone (LH) excretion 24 to 36 hours before ovulation.

Hysterosalpingogram:  A procedure performed by using fluoroscopy 2 to 5 days after cessation of menstrual flow to determine tubal patency, to show intrauterine adhesions, anomalies, filling defects, and pelvic adhesions.   If an intrauterine abnormality is found, hysteroscopy can be performed.  Hysteroscopic lysis of adhesions improves fertility.   Laparoscopy allows further evaluation of tubal function and sometimes simultaneous fulguration or laser ablation of pelvic adhesions.

Postcoital Test:   In which cervical mucus is examined after intercourse, including mucus receptivity and the ability of sperm to survive and to access the upper reproductive tract are measured.

Assisted Reproductive Techniques

In Vitro Fertilization  is used to increasingly, eg, for patients with tubal disease, endometriosis, oligospermia, sperm antibodies, and unexplained infertility.  The procedure involves ovarian hyperstimulation, oocyte retrieval, fertilization, embryo culture, and embryo transfer (to the uterine cavity).  Despite the transfer of multiple embryos (often 3-4), the average term-pregnancy rate is about 20 to 25 % per attempt.

Gamete Intrafallopian Tube Transfer (GIFT)  may be used for women who have normal tubal function and unexplained infertility or endometriosis.  Multiple oocytes and sperm are obtained as for in vitro fertilization but are transferred - laparoscopically or transvaginally with ultrasound guidance - to the distal fallopian tubes, where fertilization occurs.  Success rates are about 25 to 35% at most infertility centers.

Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection  is the primary method of achieving pregnancy when other techniques have not resulted in fertilization or when the semen analysis detects marked abnormalities.  Sperm are injected into the oocyte, bypassing sperm abnormalities that affect attachment to or penetration of the oocyte.

Case Study

Her menstruation is regular with some dark red clots and her husband's semen analysis is normal.  The hysterosalpingogram shows no abnormalities. She said she had an abortion done when she was a high schooler.  Her tongue is normal and pulses are slightly wiry, especially on Guans.
