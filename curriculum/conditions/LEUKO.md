                                                Leukorrhagia

Leukorrhagia refers to a disease characterized by persistent excessive mucous vaginal discharge.  It is called Dai Xia in TCM.
In western medicine, infections and other inflammatory disorders affecting the vaginal mucosa and sometimes the vulva, commonly producing an abnormal vaginal discharge.

Etiology and Pathogenesis

Improper diet
Kidney Qi deficiency,  loose Dai Mai
Long standing Lr Qi stagnation

A complete history including symptoms and color, consistency, odor, and duration of the discharge should be taken.  The patient is asked whether the discharge occurs in relation to menses; whether it is recurrent; whether itching, burning, pain, or a lesion is present; and what bothers her the most.
A sexual history (contraceptive use and STDs), as well as questions about urethral discharge, pruritus, postcoital irritation, vaginal infection, and whether anyone else in the household has genital itching are pertinent.

Differentiation

Clinically, dilute, clear, white leukorrhea usually accompanies with cold damp;  While thick, yellow ( or white-yellow ) leukorrhea with foul smell is regarded as a damp heat.

Western Etiology & pathology

In women of reproductive age, a normal physiologic discharge may be mistaken for an infection.  The discharge is commonly milky white or mucoid, arising from the cervix or vagina as an epithelial transudate.  Colonization of the vagina by Lactobacillus sp keeps the pH normal (3.8 to 4.2), preventing the overgrowth of bacteria and yeast.  Menstrual blood, certain infections, or semen often makes the vaginal pH alkaline.
Hygiene sprays or perfumes, menstrual pads, laundry soaps, bleaches, fabric softeners, fabric dyes, synthetic fibers, bath-water additives, and toilet tissue may cause vulvar hypersensitivity.  Tight, nonporous underclothing or poor hygiene may foster fungal and bacterial growth.  Occasionally, sensitivity to spermicides, vaginal lubricants or creams, or latex condoms or diaphragms can cause irritation, which may be confused with an infection.  Also, frequent douching with irritants can result in an overgrowth of vaginal pathogens, leading to infection.
In the reproductive years, vulvitis is usually secondary to vagianl infections, whereas in the premenarchal and postmenopausal years, vulvitis commonly occurs alone.
After menopause (occurring naturally or due to oophorectomy, pelvic radiation, or chemotherapy), estrogen deficiency causes the vaginal mucosa to thin.  Consequently, the vaginal mucosa is more vulnerable to trauma and infection.  Occasionally, thinning results in irritation without infection (atrophic vaginitis), leading to scanty and alkaline discharge.
Candidal infections may occur in diabetic women and those women who are taking hormone replacement therapy or tamoxifen.  Bacterial vaginosis is common, except in women confined to bed such as those in a nursing home.

Treatment

Physiologic discharge is annoying because of soiled clothing and the feeling of wetness, but unless itching, irritation, or malodor is present, reassurance, not treatment, is indicated.
For acute vulvitis, the cause should be eliminated; measures to reduce the irritation ( e.g. wearing loose, absorbent cotton clothing that allows air circulation; keeping the vulva clean) should be taken.  Soups should be avoided.  Topical corticosteroids reduce itching and are indicated when infection is absent.  Oral antihistamines diminish itching and cause drowsiness, helping the patient sleep.
Postmenopausal patients with atrophic vaginitis are treated with estrogen.
