Cervical  Spondylosis

Cervical spondylosis, also known as cervical spondylosis syndrome, is a common disease of middle-aged and older people.  It is caused by degenerative changes in the intervertebral disk and annulus.  Common causes of this disease include deficiency of the Liver and Kidney, and deterioration of the tendons and bones, leading to thickening of the ligaments and osteophyte formation that narrow the cervical canal or neural foramina.  Congenital deformity of the cervical vertebrae is another cause.  Congenital narrow canal is at high risk.   Sprain of the neck, chronic injury, and invasion by external wind, cold and damp may induce a attack or make the symptoms worse.

This syndrome is classified into the following types:

1.  Strained Neck Type
     Recurrent episodes of soreness at the nape, which spreads to the upper back, and limited range of the neck motion in all directions like that of a strained neck.  The soreness may improve in 3-5 days, but soon return and gradually it stays with no obvious sign of remission.  X-rays show osteophytes, a straightened physiological arc, and calcification of the ligaments.

2.  Nerve Root Type ( Pain may predominate with radicular signs in the dermatome    most affected, often between C5 and C6 or C6 and C7.)
     Compression of the root of the cervical spinal nerves due to osteophytes of the cervical vertebrae, degeneration of the intervertebral discs, loosening of the articular capsule, and stricture of the intervertebral foramen.
     Pain and numbness of the upper limb on one side.  The pain is acute and severe in the shoulder and arm, which makes the patient keep the head turned slightly toward the affected side.  The numbness mainly involves fingers, or the upper arm and forearm.  The position of the neck, shoulder and arm at sleep can make it better or worse.  Or no pronounced pain or numbness, but muscle strength of the affected limb is weakened, or even muscular atrophy of the greater and lesser thenar occurs in a prolonged case.

3.  Spinal Cord Type (If spinal cord compression occurs, progressive myelopathy,
                                  especially with development of a spastic gait, is typical.)
     Spinal cord compression causes hyperreflexia, increased muscle tone, vibratory impairment, and plantar extensor responses in the legs.
     A slow onset with weakness, numbness and coldness of the lower limbs, and inflexibility in walking (spastic gait). Better with treatment and rest, but Sx return and gradually become worse.

4.  Vertebral Artery Type
     Dizziness and vertigo, or accompanied by distending pain in the forehead.  A quick neck motion may make the dizziness and vertigo worse. Or accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and unsteady walking;  or tinnitus and impaired hearing.

5.  Sympathetic Nerve Type
     Profuse sweating and flushed skin;  or eyelids weakness, distending pain in the eyes, lacrimation;  or impaired hearing and tinnitus;  or discomfort in the throat, and nausea.

Western medicine perspective

Diagnosis
Spinal x-ray, including oblique views of the neural foramina, show degenerative changes with osteophytes and disk space narrowing.  CT defines canal diameter(C canal<10 mm), however, MRI is the best.

Treatment
Conservative therapy includes a soft collar, anti-inflammatory drugs, muscle relaxants, and mild analgesics.  Decompressive laminectomy is indicated if the patient has a myelopathy and cord compressive confirmed by imaging studies.
