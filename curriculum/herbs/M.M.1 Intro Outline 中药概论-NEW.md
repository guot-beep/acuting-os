                    Origins of Chinese Herbal Medicine

The development of Chinese herbal medicine was a long and gradual process. Primitive
man, in the search for food from plants and by hunting animals, discovered medicinal
substances. The accumulation of experiences during this search eventually developed into
the earliest knowledge of Chinese medicine.

"Doctor" Recorded in Written History
The term "doctor" was first recorded in a Chinese history book dating back to 1066-771 B.C.

    • Shi Jing (诗经) recorded Chinese herbs in 221 B.C.
    • Shan Hai Jing (山海经) recorded over 100 animal or botanical herbal substances.

Divine Farmer's Classic of Materia Medica (Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing)
    • The earliest extant treatise on Chinese medicine and herbs.
    • Written during the Eastern (later) Han Dynasty (A.D. 25-220).
    • Composed of 3 volumes.
    • Lists 365 herbal substances (252 botanical, 45 mineral, 67 zoological).
    • Introduced key concepts:
             o Temperature (Si Qi 四气).
             o Taste (Wu Wei 五味).
             o Toxicity.
             o Medicinal action of each herb.

Development of the Materia Medica
Tang Dynasty 618-907 CE

    • Tang Materia Medica (唐本草) was published under the Tang government in 659 CE.
    • Contained 844 herbs.
    • The first illustrated materia medica.

Song Dynasty 960-1279 CE
    • Tai Ping Hui Min He Ji Ju Fang (和剂局方).
    • Developed the concepts of Eighteen Incompatibilities and Nineteen Antagonisms.

Ming Dynasty 1368-1644 CE
    • Li Shi Zhen (1518-1593 CE), China's most celebrated herbalist:
             o Started practice in 1542.
             o Traveled widely, collecting and tasting herbs.
             o Took 27 years to write a monumental book.
             o Includes 1,892 individual herbal substances, 11,000 illustrations, and over
                  10,000 herbal formulas.

                    Processing and Preparing Herbal Substances

Purpose
    • To eliminate or decrease toxins, side effects, or the intensity of an herbal substance.
    • To modify the actions of herbs to fit specific clinical conditions.
    • To ease storage.
    • To clean and prepare herbs for medicinal use.

                                  Methods of Processing and Preparing
    1. Mechanical Methods

             o Cleaning, sorting, sifting, scraping, peeling, slicing, and winnowing.
    2. Methods Using Water

            o 润 (Moistening): Softening the herb before slicing.
            o 漂 (Soaking): Washing herbs to remove unwanted properties.
            o 水飞 (Aqueous Trituration): Grinding minerals with water until reduced to a

                  fine powder.
    3. Methods Using Fire

            o Dry-frying (炒 chao).
                      1. Dry the herbs for storage or to more easily break them down to small
                           pieces.
                      2. Dirt and wheat can be used to alter the actions of herbs.

            o Frying with liquids (炙 zhi).
                      1. Honey: tonifies and moisturizes
                      2. Wine: Invigorates blood and alleviates pain
                      3. Ginger: Antidote; warms and soothes the stomach
                      4. Vinegar: smooths the liver and alleviates pain
                      5. Salt: Tonifies the kidneys

            o Calcining (煅 duan).
                      1. Adds astringent action, rendering the substance brittle and easy to
                           pulverize.
                      2. Usually applied to minerals and shells.

            o Roasting in ashes (煨 wei).

                                     Decoctions (汤 tang)

    • The most common form of traditional Chinese medicine preparation in China.
    • Made in ceramic or earthenware pots.
    • Water is the primary solvent; wine and vinegar can also be used as solvents.
    • Types of Heat:

            o Military Fire (武火 wu huo): High flame.
            o Civilian Fire (文火 wen huo): Low flame.

Cooking Method of Decoction

    1. Soak the herbs for 30 minutes to 1 hour.
    2. Once boiling, reduce the heat.
    3. Avoid lifting the lid frequently.
    4. Most formulas are cooked for 20–30 minutes.

             o Formulas that release the exterior or contain aromatic herbs: Cook for 15–20
                  minutes.

             o Tonic or cloying herbs: Cook for 45–60 minutes.
    5. Decoct the herbs twice.
    6. Decoctions are typically consumed warm, twice a day.

Special Considerations for Decoction

    • Decocted First (先煎 xian jian):
             o Toxic herbs: Cook for 30–45 minutes.
             o Minerals and shells: Cook for 20–30 minutes.

    • Decocted in Gauze (包煎 bao jian):
             o Herbs with cilia (fine, hair-like structures), small seeds, or certain minerals
                  should be wrapped in gauze or cheesecloth.

                     Characteristics of Chinese Herbal Medicine

One of the strengths of Chinese Herbal Medicine (CHM) lies in its ability to prescribe unique
formulas based on a precise understanding of the symptom pattern and the nature and
properties of individual herbs. This approach enables the formulation of a prescription that
addresses specific symptom patterns by utilizing herbs with tailored characteristics and
actions.

Characteristics refer to:
    • Thermal property (四气 si qi).
    • Taste (五味 wu wei).
    • Direction (升降浮沉 sheng jiang fu chen).
    • Channel affiliation (归经 gui jing).
    • Toxicity (毒性 du xing), if any.

Direction (升降浮沉 sheng jiang fu chen)
    • Upward (升 sheng), Downward (降 jiang), Outward (浮 fu), Inward (沉 chen).

Directions and properties are connected

    • Herbs with acrid, sweet, or hot properties tend to go upward and outward.
    • Herbs with sour, bitter, salty, or cold properties move downward and inward.
    • Direction can be affected by special treatment of the herbs, such as preparing with

         wine, ginger, vinegar, or salt.

Channel Affiliation (归经 gui jing)
    • Refers to the specific function and indications of herbs, such as Xing Ren for the
         Lung and Bu Gu Zhi for treating diarrhea due to Spleen and Kidney deficiency.

Toxicity (毒性 du xing)
    • Some herbs have toxic effects if not used properly. Most herbs are safe when used
         correctly and within recommended doses.

                                 Herb-to-Herb Interactions

Seven Features

    1. Dan Xing 单 行 (Single Effect): Using a single herb for a simple condition.
             • Huang Qin treats cough due to mild Lung Heat

    2. Xiang Xu 相 须 (Mutual Accentuation): Herbs with similar functions enhance the
         overall therapeutic effect.
             • Shi Gao + Zhi Mu
             • Da Huang + Mang Xiao

    3. Xiang Shi 相 使 (Mutual Enhancement): Herbs with different functions enhance each
         other's properties.
             • Fu Ling + Huang Qi
             • Huang Qin + Da Huang

    4. Xiang Wei 相 畏 (Mutual Counteraction): One herb neutralizes or minimizes the toxic
         aspect of another.
             • Sheng Jiang + Ban Xia

As a general rule, herbs with potent effects to regulate qi, move blood or drain downward
should be avoided during pregnancy. These herbs are classified as contraindicated or for
use with caution in the treatment of pregnant women.

Dietary Consideration
Foods that are cold, raw, greasy, rotten, or spoiled, and other foods that are difficult to
digest, should be avoided when taking herbal medicines.

    1. Chang Shan (Radix Dichroae)—no onion
    2. Sheng or Shu Di Huang, He Shou Wu - no garlic or onion
    3. Bo He – no crab
    4. Fu Ling – no vinegar
    5. Cong Bai (green onion)- no Honey

Measurement and Dosing
    • 1 Liang = 30 g
    • 1 Qian = 3 g
    • 1 Fen = 0.3 g
    • 5-10 g is a common dosage for most single herbs in a formula. That is the daily dose
         for an adult of average height and weight when using dry herbs. Some herbs can be
         used at higher doses of 15-30 g.

Measurement and Dosing
    • Use ¼ of the adult dosage for children 5 years old or under.
    • Use ½ of the adult dosage for children 6 years or older.
    • Patients with a weak constitution need to be given low doses of herbs.
    • Use a smaller dosage with light herbs.
    • Use a bigger dosage with heavy herbs.
