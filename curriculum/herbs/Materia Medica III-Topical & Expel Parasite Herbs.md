SECTION 1

Herbs That Expel Parasites

Category Overview

Herbs that expel parasites are primarily used to treat intestinal parasitic infections.
According to Bensky, these herbs must be carefully matched to the type of parasite, the
patient's constitution, and the presence of accumulation or deficiency patterns.
These herbs often:

    � kill or expel intestinal parasites
    � move accumulation downward
    � alleviate abdominal pain
    � address accompanying qi stagnation or damp obstruction
Typical symptoms of parasitic infection include:
    � peri-umbilical abdominal pain
    � appetite changes
    � vomiting
    � anal itching
    � fatigue with emaciation

Global Characteristics

Characteristic                         Description

Taste           Often bitter or acrid

Temperature     Variable (often warm or cold)

Bing Lang ()

Semen Arecae Catechu | Betel nut
Nature & Flavor: Acrid, Bitter, Warm
Channels: Stomach, Large Intestine, Spleen, Liver

Primary Actions

    1. Kills parasites (especially tapeworms)
    2. Drains downward and reduces accumulation
    3. Moves qi and reduces abdominal distention
    4. Promotes urination and reduces edema

Key Indications

    � Tapeworm infestation
    � Abdominal distention with food stagnation
    � Constipation due to accumulation
    � Damp leg qi with edema

Cautions/Contraindications

    � Use cautiously in Spleen deficiency
    � Avoid in pregnancy

Bing Lang = the strongest parasite herb that ALSO moves qi downward.

Clinical Insight

Bing Lang is both an antiparasitic and a qi-moving herb, making it useful when parasites
coexist with food stagnation or abdominal distention.
Bing Lang strongly drives parasites downward and is therefore often combined with other
herbs that weaken parasites.

Da Fu Pi ()

Pericarpium Arecae | Betel nut husk
Nature & Flavor: Acrid, Slightly Warm
Channels: Spleen, Stomach, Large Intestine, Small Intestine

Primary Actions

    1. Moves qi and alleviates abdominal distention
    2. Promotes urination and reduces edema

Key Indications

    � Abdominal distention due to qi stagnation
    � Edema from damp obstruction
    � Damp accumulation with qi stagnation

Cautions/Contraindications

    � Use cautiously in qi deficiency
    � Not a true antiparasitic herb despite its botanical relationship with Bing Lang

Preparation

    � Usually decocted
    � Sometimes added later to preserve aromatic qi-moving action

Dosage

6�12 g decoction

Exam Pearl

Da Fu Pi treats damp obstruction with qi stagnation, while Bing Lang directly expels

Although related botanically to Bing Lang, Da Fu Pi is primarily a qi-moving herb rather than
a direct antiparasitic herb.

SECTION 2

Topical Application Herbs

Category Overview

Topical application of herbs is primarily used to treat external disorders affecting the skin,
tissues, and localized areas of the body. These herbs act through direct absorption into the
skin and local tissues.
They are commonly used to treat:

    � skin infections
    � itching disorders
    � parasites and fungi
    � traumatic injuries
    � chronic ulcers or wounds
Many topical herbs are toxic and must be used carefully.

Forms of Application

Common preparation methods include:
Topical preparations

    � powders
    � ointments
    � pastes
    � plasters
    � creams
External liquid preparations
    � herbal washes
    � decoction washes
    � mouth gargles
    � steam inhalation
Local instillation
    � eye drops
    � ear drops
